# Understanding Audio Formats: MP3, WAV, FLAC, OGG, and M4A Explained

Choosing the right audio format can be confusing. Should you use MP3 or WAV? What’s FLAC? Is OGG still relevant? This guide breaks down the most common audio formats, their pros and cons, and when to use each – plus how Dayront makes conversion effortless.

## The Two Families: Lossy vs Lossless

All audio formats fall into two categories:

- **Lossless** – preserves every bit of the original recording. Examples: WAV, FLAC, ALAC.
- **Lossy** – discards some audio data to reduce file size. Examples: MP3, AAC (M4A), OGG Vorbis.

### Lossless Formats

**WAV (Waveform Audio File Format)**
- Uncompressed, perfect quality.
- Large file size (~10 MB per minute of stereo CD quality).
- Ideal for editing, archiving, and mastering.

**FLAC (Free Lossless Audio Codec)**
- Compressed but lossless – about 50% of WAV size.
- Open‑source, widely supported (except iTunes).
- Perfect for music collections and archiving.

### Lossy Formats

**MP3 (MPEG‑1 Audio Layer III)**
- The most universal format.
- Good quality at 192‑320 kbps; near‑transparent at 256+ kbps.
- Supported everywhere.

**AAC / M4A (Advanced Audio Coding)**
- Better quality than MP3 at the same bitrate.
- Used by iTunes, YouTube, and iPhones.
- Files usually have `.m4a` or `.aac` extension.

**OGG Vorbis**
- Open‑source, royalty‑free.
- Excellent quality, often smaller than MP3.
- Popular in games (e.g., Minecraft) and streaming.

## Which Format Should You Choose?

**If you’re editing:** Use WAV or FLAC. Lossy formats degrade each time you re‑encode them.

**If you’re distributing a podcast:** MP3 at 192 kbps is the standard. AAC (M4A) offers better quality at the same size.

**If you want to save phone storage:** Convert your FLAC music library to MP3 320 kbps or M4A 256 kbps.

**If you need maximum compatibility:** MP3.
